<p className="lede">
I have a confession. One of my favorite things to do in trading — and I know how this is going to sound — is to sit there with an open position and repeatedly fiddle with my profit target. Nudge it up. Nudge it down. Drag it to the next resistance level. Drag it back.
</p>

There is some part of me that believes, in a way no rational adult should admit out loud, that the act of fussing with the target is somehow *connected* to the trade working out. Like talking to a houseplant to help it grow. I do not actually think it does anything. But I do it anyway, and it makes me happy.

I want to spend a minute on this, because it turns out the psychology literature has a lot to say about why I do it, and whether I should stop.

## Skinner's pigeons, briefly, with apologies

In 1948, the behaviorist B. F. Skinner ran what is probably the most useful experiment ever published for understanding trader behavior. He put hungry pigeons in a cage with a mechanism that dropped food pellets at random intervals — no matter what the pigeon did.

Within minutes, the pigeons developed elaborate routines. One bobbed its head in a specific rhythm. Another spun counterclockwise between feedings. A third pecked at a particular corner of the cage. Every bird was convinced, in whatever way a pigeon is convinced of anything, that its specific routine was causing the food to appear.

Skinner called it *superstitious behavior.* The food was arriving randomly. The pigeons' nervous systems could not accept that. They needed a story in which their actions mattered.

## Ellen Langer does it with humans

Ellen Langer, at Harvard in the 1970s, ran a more polite version of the same experiment on humans. She gave subjects lottery tickets — some got to pick their own number, some had one assigned. Then she tried to buy the tickets back before the drawing. The people who had picked their own numbers demanded, on average, four times more money than the people who had been assigned a number.

Same odds. Same random draw. But the act of *choosing* made them feel like they had some hand in the outcome. Langer called this the *illusion of control*, and she showed it again and again — with dice throws, with stock picks, with coin tosses — always in situations where the outcome was actually random.

Traders are basically Skinner's pigeons in slightly better clothes.

The market delivers wins and losses at intervals that feel patterned but are, at the short-term level, overwhelmingly noise. So we develop rituals. We nudge the profit target. We redraw the trendline. We check the chart on a different timeframe. We switch to the other monitor. Somewhere underneath all of that, the same nervous-system bargain is being made: *if I keep attending to this, it will reward me.*

## When the ritual is harmless, and when it isn't

Now, here is where I have to be honest about the dark side, because some of you are trading right now, and I do not want you to take the wrong lesson.

Fiddling with the profit target is harmless in exactly one condition: *when the fiddle stays inside the plan you had when you entered the trade.* If you entered long at 42 with a target at 48, and you want to bump the target to 48.25 because there is a cleaner level there, fine. That's ritual. That's the head-bob.

Fiddling becomes self-sabotage the moment it violates the reason you entered the trade.

Moving the target *closer* because you are nervous — "I'll just take what's there" — is the disposition effect from yesterday's essay, dressed up as discipline. Moving the stop *further away* because the trade has not worked yet is even worse. Brett Steenbarger, who has coached more professional traders than almost anyone alive, writes about this constantly: the question is not *are you making adjustments.* The question is *whose voice is making them* — the plan's, or the fear's. Same keystrokes. Different trades entirely.

<Pullquote>
Bobbing your head while the pellets fall is fine. Bobbing harder is the problem.
</Pullquote>

My own fiddling — the head-bob kind — does not change my risk. If it ever starts changing my risk, that is my signal to close the platform and go for a walk.

## The market is a pigeon too

Here is what is interesting, and it is the part I actually want you to leave with.

The behavior I am describing in myself — the inability to just sit still and let a thing play out — is the same behavior that creates the best trading opportunities on the chart. Because the market as a whole is a giant collective of people who cannot sit still either. They pile into things that are rising. They abandon things that are falling. And both moves overshoot.

Werner De Bondt and Richard Thaler, in a paper that basically launched behavioral finance as an academic field, showed in 1985 that stocks with the worst three-to-five-year returns subsequently outperformed stocks with the best three-to-five-year returns by roughly 25 percentage points over the following three years. The losers became winners. The winners became losers. Not because the companies magically changed, but because investors had overreacted in both directions, and the pendulum eventually swung back.

Jegadeesh and Titman showed the complementary effect on shorter timeframes — momentum works for six to twelve months, and then reverses.

So the pattern, summarized and probably oversimplified: overshoot up, overshoot down, revert. Rise hard, fall hard. The higher you stretch the rubber band, the harder it snaps back.

## Facebook, the Aussie, the pound

Facebook, right now, is a stretched rubber band. I am not short it yet. I will make a big deal about it when I am. But the anatomy of a three-hundred-billion-dollar company that has risen this fast is the same anatomy De Bondt and Thaler were writing about — expectations have outrun fundamentals, and every marginal buyer is someone who thinks the next buyer will pay more. When that assumption breaks, the fall is faster than anyone models in advance.

On the other side: the Australian dollar has been beaten senseless. The British pound, on one recent Monday session, collapsed on a Brexit poll and then rocketed back when the next poll flipped.

These are the rubber bands stretched the other way. When they recover, they recover violently. That's the trade. Not predicting *when* the snap back starts — nobody does that well — but being ready when it does.

## Two things to do with this

First: know which category your fiddling falls into. If you are nudging the target to feel engaged, and your risk is unchanged, fine. Bob your head. You are a pigeon and so am I. If you are moving stops and targets because fear is driving, you need a hard rule — write it on the monitor — that says *no adjustments after entry that were not in the plan before entry.*

Second: turn the same attention outward. The market's fiddling is what creates the opportunities. Look for the stocks and currencies that have been stretched farthest in either direction. Not the ones that look boring. The ones that look crazy. Those are the rubber bands. That is where the trades live.
