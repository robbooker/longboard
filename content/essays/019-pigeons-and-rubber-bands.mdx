<p className="lede">
OK, confession time. One of my favorite things to do in trading — and I am aware of how this is going to sound — is to sit at the screen with an open position and repeatedly fiddle with my profit target. Nudge it up. Nudge it down. Drag it to the next resistance level. Drag it back to the old one. Sometimes I do this four or five times in a row while the price hasn't moved at all.
</p>

There's some part of me that believes, in a way no adult with a job is supposed to admit out loud, that the act of fussing with the target is *connected* to the trade working out. Like talking to a plant. I do not, in any rigorous sense, think it does anything. I do it anyway, and it makes me feel better.

I want to spend a minute on this, because it turns out the psychology literature has a lot to say about why I do it (a lot) and whether I should stop (also a lot, it's complicated).

## Skinner's pigeons, briefly, with apologies

In 1948 B. F. Skinner ran what is, on the list of most useful experiments ever published for understanding retail trader behavior, somewhere around first. He put hungry pigeons in a cage with a mechanism that dropped food pellets at random intervals. It was random. The pigeons did not know it was random, because the pigeons were pigeons.

Within minutes the pigeons started doing things. One bobbed its head in a specific rhythm. Another spun counterclockwise between feedings. A third pecked at a particular corner of the cage, which had no special property except that it was a corner and the pigeon liked it. Every bird was convinced — in whatever sense a pigeon is convinced of anything — that its particular routine was what was causing the food to appear.

Skinner called this *superstitious behavior.* You can see where this is going.

## The more polite version, with humans

A generation later, Ellen Langer at Harvard ran the same experiment on people, except she used lotteries instead of pellets, which is more dignified. She gave subjects lottery tickets. Some got to pick their own number; some had one assigned. Then, before the drawing, she tried to buy the tickets back.

The people who had picked their own numbers wanted, on average, about four times more money than the people who'd been given a number.

Same drawing. Same odds. Same random mechanism, enforced by state law. The act of *choosing* made people feel like they had some hand in the outcome, and so the ticket was worth four times more to them, in a coherent market where the ticket was worth exactly the same amount to everyone. Langer called this the *illusion of control*, and she repeated the experiment with dice, coin flips, and stock picks, and it kept coming out the same way.

Traders are basically Skinner's pigeons in slightly better clothes.

The market delivers wins and losses at intervals that look patterned and are, at the short-term level, mostly noise. Our nervous systems cannot accept this, because our nervous systems are pigeon-adjacent. So we develop rituals. We nudge the profit target. We redraw the trendline. We check the chart on a different timeframe. We switch to the other monitor. We open a news tab. Somewhere underneath all of it, the same bargain is being made, which is: *if I keep attending to this, it will reward me.*

It will not. Or rather, it might, but not because of the attending.

## When the ritual is fine, and when it is the actual problem

I have to be honest about this part, because some of you are trading right now, and if you take the wrong lesson I'll feel bad.

Fiddling with your profit target is harmless in exactly one condition: *when the fiddle stays inside the plan you had when you entered the trade.* You entered long at 42 with a target at 48. You want to bump the target to 48.25 because there's a cleaner level there. Fine. That's ritual. That's the head-bob. The pigeon is not harming the pigeon.

Fiddling becomes self-sabotage the moment it violates the reason you entered the trade.

Moving the target *closer* because you are nervous — "I'll just take what's there, I'm being disciplined, look at me" — is the disposition effect from an earlier issue, wearing a mustache. Moving the stop *further away* because the trade hasn't worked yet is worse, and I will not belabor why. Brett Steenbarger, who has coached more professional traders than probably anyone alive, writes about this constantly. The question, he says, is not *are you making adjustments.* The question is *whose voice is making them* — the plan's, or the fear's. Same keystrokes. Completely different trades.

<Pullquote>
Bobbing your head while the pellets fall is fine. Bobbing harder when the pellets don't come is the problem.
</Pullquote>

My fiddling is the head-bob kind. It does not change my risk. If it ever starts changing my risk, that is my cue to close the platform and go for a walk, because the pigeon has got loose.

## The market is also a pigeon

Here's what's interesting, and this is actually the part I want you to leave with.

The behavior I've just described in myself — the inability to sit still while a thing plays out — is exactly the behavior that creates the best trading opportunities on the chart. Because the market, in the aggregate, is a very large pigeon. It piles into things that are rising. It abandons things that are falling. And both moves overshoot.

Werner De Bondt and Richard Thaler, in a 1985 paper that more or less invented behavioral finance as an academic field, showed that stocks with the worst three-to-five-year returns subsequently outperformed stocks with the best three-to-five-year returns by roughly 25 percentage points over the following three years. That's not rounding error. That's a career, if you could do it reliably, which basically nobody can, which is a separate essay.

Why did this happen? Not because the companies magically changed. Because investors had overreacted in both directions, and the pendulum eventually swung back, because pendulums do. Jegadeesh and Titman showed the complementary effect on shorter timeframes — momentum works for six to twelve months, and then reverses, also because pendulums do.

So the shape of what markets do, roughly, is: overshoot up, overshoot down, revert, repeat. Rise hard, fall hard. The higher the rubber band is stretched, the harder it snaps back.

This is depressing if you were hoping for something more sophisticated. Markets have been written about for hundreds of years by people with much more impressive credentials than mine and the takeaway is still *they overreact and then they don't.*

## Facebook, the Aussie, the pound

Facebook, at the moment I'm writing this, is a stretched rubber band. I am not short it yet. I will make a very big deal about it when I am. But the anatomy of a three-hundred-billion-dollar company that has risen this far, this fast, is the same anatomy De Bondt and Thaler were writing about in 1985 — expectations have outrun fundamentals, and every marginal buyer is someone who thinks the next buyer will pay more. When that assumption breaks — and these assumptions always break, eventually, because they are structurally absurd — the fall is faster than anyone models in advance. The pigeons all leave at once.

On the other side: the Australian dollar has been beaten senseless for no particularly good reason. The British pound, on one recent Monday session, collapsed on a Brexit poll and then rocketed back when the next poll flipped, which is not, you know, a fundamental event. These are rubber bands stretched the other way. When they recover, they recover violently.

That's the trade. Not predicting *when* the snap starts — nobody does that well, including people who say they do — but being organized enough to show up when it does.

## Two things to take away, and that's it

First: know which category your own fiddling falls into. If you are nudging the target because you cannot stand to just sit there and watch, and your risk is unchanged, fine. Bob your head. You're a pigeon and so am I and so, frankly, is everyone I know in this business. If you are moving stops and targets because fear is driving, you need a hard rule, written somewhere you can see it, that says *no adjustments after entry that were not in the plan before entry.* Write it on the monitor. Masking tape is fine. Nobody's going to see it but you.

Second, and this is the one I actually want you to remember: turn the same attention outward. The market's fiddling is what creates the opportunities. Look for the stocks and currencies that have been stretched farthest in either direction. Not the boring ones. The ones that look crazy, that everyone has strong feelings about, that make the news. Those are the rubber bands. That is where the trades live.
