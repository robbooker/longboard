Look, there's a version of trading advice that sounds profound and is actually just a polite way of saying "you're doing the same thing over and over again."

Anyway.

I was listening to an old recording of myself the other day — which is always a slightly humbling experience, like reading your own emails from 2012 or looking at a haircut you once thought was defensible — and the basic message was embarrassingly consistent with what I still say now: the problem is not that you don't know what to do. The problem is that you keep doing something else instead.

And I mean *keep.*

## § I. The trade behind the trade

The thing about most losing trades is that they are not really about the setup, or the catalyst, or the chart, or the news, or whatever explanation you prefer this week.

They are about the second decision.

You probably know this part. You enter a trade. It goes against you. And then there is a moment — a very small, very quiet moment — where you decide what kind of trader you are going to be.

And then you decide incorrectly.

Not in a dramatic way. Not in a "this will ruin my life" way. Just in a small, procedural, almost boring way. You add when you said you wouldn't. You hold when you said you'd cut. You resize the position in your head instead of in your account.

This is the trade behind the trade. And it is the only one that matters.

## § II. What the research says, briefly, with apologies

There's a whole research literature on this, which I will summarize briefly and probably badly.

Daniel Kahneman wrote a very famous book about it called *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (FSG, 2011), which you have either read or you haven't; it doesn't really matter. The short version is that you have two decision-making systems. One is fast, automatic, pattern-matching, and emotional. The other is slow, effortful, and deliberative. Academics call them System 1 and System 2, which is not a great name but fine.

<Pullquote>
  The problem is not that you don't know what to do. The problem is that you keep doing something else instead.
</Pullquote>

The trouble, for traders, is that the first decision — the one where you pick the setup, size the position, set the stop — happens when System 2 is in charge. Coffee in hand, screen at rest, nothing yet on the line. The second decision — the one where the trade is going against you and your stop is nine cents away — happens when System 1 has taken over and System 2 is somewhere in the break room. You are now a different person deciding with a different brain, which sounds dramatic but is approximately what Kahneman's forty years of research describes.

Charles Duhigg covers the adjacent territory in *The Power of Habit* (Random House, 2012), which is mostly about how repeated behaviors eventually stop going through the decision-making part of the brain at all. They become automatic. This is the entire game, by the way. The question is not *whether* your behavior will become automatic — it will — but *which* behavior.

## § III. Yes, I know

I mean, I know how this sounds.

It sounds like the kind of advice that gets printed on a coffee mug and then ignored. "Cut your losses." "Stick to your plan." "Trade smaller." (The correct answer is to trade smaller, which is also the correct answer to most questions about trading.)

But here is the annoying part: the simplicity is not the problem. The repetition is the problem.

Markets are repetitive. Setups are repetitive. Mistakes are repetitive. You are repetitive.

Which is good, in theory. Repetition is how you learn things.

Except — and this is the part that is less fun — you are also very good at repeating the wrong thing.

## § IV. What you think you're doing

Right, so.

Most traders think they are making a series of independent decisions. This trade, that trade, a new opportunity, a fresh start. A kind of episodic narrative where each trade is its own story.

It is not. It is one long trade.

Richard Thaler — whose book *Misbehaving* (Norton, 2015) won him a Nobel Prize, which is not a thing I can say about this essay — has written extensively about the human tendency to treat repeated decisions as one-offs. People are terrible at treating repeated decisions as part of a system and very good at treating them as isolated events. Which is great if you are buying shoes and less great if you are risking capital.

Because if every trade is "new," then every mistake is also "new," which means it never quite counts as a pattern.

And if it's not a pattern, you don't have to fix it.

## § V. The part where I tell you what to do

So here is the part where I am supposed to give you the answer.

The answer is to make the second decision automatic.

Not better. Not smarter. Not more disciplined in some abstract, aspirational way. Just automatic.

You decide in advance what happens when the trade goes wrong, and then you remove your ability to improvise.

Because improvisation is where all the damage happens.

And I realize this sounds restrictive. It is restrictive. That is the point. A system is mostly a structure for removing the improvisations you'd otherwise commit under duress.

(Traders love freedom. The market does not care.)

## § VI. The market is not subtle

Here's a fact.

The market is embarrassingly repetitive, which is a gift, although it doesn't feel like one. The same types of moves happen over and over again — the same traps, the same squeezes, the same slow fades, the same violent reversals.

And, correspondingly, the same mistakes.

Chasing. Averaging. Hoping. Rationalizing. Rewriting the thesis mid-trade so that it matches the current pain level.

You can dress these up in different language if you want — macro backdrop, liquidity conditions, sentiment shifts — but mostly they are just the same behavior wearing a slightly different hat.

Which means you don't need a new insight.

You need a closed door.

## § VII. The quiet part

The quiet part of all this — the part that doesn't make it into most trading advice — is that recognizing the mistake is not the same as fixing it.

In fact, recognizing the mistake is often what allows you to keep making it.

Because now you have a narrative. You can say "I know I shouldn't be doing this" while continuing to do it. Which feels like progress, but isn't.

The lesson keeps following you because your process leaves the same door open. And the market, being repetitive and not particularly sentimental, will keep walking through it.

Trade smaller.

Close the door.

Remove the option.

Respect the second decision.

Do it again tomorrow.

*Anyway. See you next week.*
