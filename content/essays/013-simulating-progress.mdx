<p className="lede">
Look, everybody already knows trading can be addictive. This is the cinematic version — traders glued to screens, refreshing quotes, euphoric in winning streaks, miserable in drawdowns, arranging their whole emotional life around candles and closing bells. It is not a new observation. You have probably made it yourself about someone you know, or about yourself at two in the morning.
</p>

The more interesting idea is subtler, and it is this: sometimes the person is not addicted to trading. Sometimes they are addicted to the *feeling of working on trading.*

That is a different thing.

## The trap that looks exactly like the work

The trouble is that one activity can look almost identical to the other from the outside, and worse, from the inside. A person sits in front of a screen for five hours. They test strategies. They optimize parameters. They study charts candle by candle. They rewrite rules, rerun systems, tweak the same setup endlessly, and walk away feeling intensely productive. Serious. Disciplined. Involved, they'd say, in the exact kind of work a trader ought to be doing.

The problem is that none of that is necessarily trading. It is *work-adjacent* activity, and work-adjacent activity has a reward structure all its own.

Backtesting, system design, optimization, endless refinement — these are not fake activities. They are real. Sometimes they are useful. Sometimes they are essential. But they can also become emotionally self-sealing. Every time a result flashes up on the screen, every time the equity curve suddenly looks beautiful after one more small adjustment, every time a simulated trade resolves in your favor, the brain gets a little hit of satisfaction. And the person starts to confuse the emotional reward of discovery with the economic reality of making money.

That confusion is where the problem begins.

## The part about dopamine, briefly, with apologies

Modern neuroscience has spent years trying to clarify the role of dopamine in reward, and the short version — which I will summarize briefly and probably badly — is not that dopamine equals pleasure. The story is more complicated. But dopamine is deeply involved in reward prediction and salience, particularly when outcomes are uncertain.

Backtesting contains, with unnerving precision, exactly the kinds of micro-events that keep this machinery lit up. Uncertainty. Resolution. Intermittent success. Novelty. Near-misses. The feeling of *one more run.* The quiet possibility that the next parameter tweak is the one that unlocks something brilliant.

This is why the analogy to poker and to video games is so close. The reward is not just the money. The reward is the intermittent revelation. Turn over the next card. Run the next test. Change the next setting. See what happens. Repeat.

And repetition is where a serious professional activity quietly becomes behavioral self-medication.

The market does not care how satisfying the search felt.

## Simulation sits too close to the real thing

There is a second layer to the trap, and it is worse than the first. The process is not only rewarding. It *masquerades as progress.*

In most professions the separation is obvious. A truck driver does not finish her workday and then sit in the parked cab pretending to drive imaginary roads for emotional stimulation. A pilot uses a simulator to train, yes, but if she started spending all day in the simulator while telling herself she was actively transporting passengers, the category error would be visible from space.

Trading makes this harder because simulation sits unusually close to the thing itself. A backtest *looks* like market engagement. A rules engine *looks* like a system. Optimization *looks* like discipline. The whole apparatus can feel like the trading job even when no trade has been placed, no risk has been taken, and no money has changed hands.

Which is to say: the simulator and the cockpit are, visually, almost indistinguishable. That is the problem.

## Workaholism is a technical term and it's not flattering

Researchers who study work addiction have tried for years to get people to understand that workaholism is not just working hard. Working hard is fine. Working hard is, frankly, encouraged in most of the societies that have thought about the question. Workaholism is something different. It is compulsive overinvolvement in work-like activity to the point that other parts of life are squeezed out and control deteriorates.

The diagnostic question, stripped of jargon, is simply: can you stop?

Can you leave the screen. Can you go to dinner. Can you be present with your family. Can you refrain from one more optimization pass once the returns to tweaking have obviously gone flat. Can you tell the difference between structured preparation and compulsive involvement.

If you can, then the backtesting is probably fine. If you can't, it is probably not.

And notice that this framing is agnostic on whether backtesting is a good use of your time. That's a separate question. The question here is whether *you can choose not to do it right now.*

## Two different jobs that look like one job

Here is where this gets practically useful, and it is the move the whole essay has been waiting to make.

What is the job you are actually doing?

If the job is building, testing, packaging, and maybe selling trading systems, then a lot of obsessive experimentation is not a pathology. It is the work. It may still need some boundaries so it does not eat your life, but the activity and the role at least agree with each other.

The danger — the *specific* danger — is mistaking that role for the role of a trader whose primary task is to make money by executing trades and managing risk in live markets. Those are not the same profession. They can actually pull in opposite directions.

The system builder is rewarded for novelty, creativity, exploration, invention, and the emotional high of making the thing a little more elegant. The trader is rewarded for roughly the opposite traits: tolerance for monotony, repetition, consistency, smaller menus, fewer changes, fewer opportunities for self-expression. One role feels exciting. The other feels plain. The mistake is assuming that because one role feels more stimulating, it must also be the one that pays.

It often isn't. Frequently the reverse.

## A brief, unflattering paper about backtests

While we are here, it is worth mentioning that the finance research on backtest overfitting is, to put it gently, not reassuring. Studies have repeatedly shown how easy it is to create strategies that look excellent in historical simulation and then fail in practice. This does not happen only because the researcher was technically unskilled. It happens because the process of repeated testing invites false discovery. A person tweaks, reruns, selects, and refines until the system flatters them. The more intoxicating the testing process becomes, the easier it is to forget that the market, as mentioned, does not care how satisfying the search felt.

## The part where I tell you what to do

So what's the practical takeaway?

Not "never backtest." Not "never build." Not "every enthusiastic trader is an addict." That would be an overreaction, and overreaction is its own form of not paying attention.

The better takeaway is this: be honest about which part is rewarding you.

If the reward is mostly the feeling of creation, you may be a system designer more than a trader. That is a fine thing to be. It is just not the same thing.

If the reward is mostly the rush of optimization, you may be attached to discovery theater more than to execution. Also fine, also a thing — but again, not the same thing.

And if the reward is mostly the intermittent burst of simulated success, then what you have is a habit loop with only a loose connection to actual profit. That's the one to watch.

In none of these cases is the answer shame. The answer is naming the activity correctly and then deciding what you want to do with it.

## This is broader than markets, as these things usually are

Plenty of people become addicted not to outcomes but to the rituals *around* outcomes. Planning instead of shipping. Researching instead of deciding. Configuring instead of operating. Preparing instead of performing. Markets are just a particularly honest mirror because they attach numbers to the illusion, and the numbers are public.

So the honest question is not quite "am I addicted?"

The honest question is: *what part, exactly, is giving me the reward?*

If the answer is the work-adjacent loop and not the work itself, the next move is not guilt. It is clarity.
