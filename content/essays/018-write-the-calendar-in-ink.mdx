<p className="lede">
There are two things I want you to take from this one, and they are going to sound like they come from different universes. The first is a trading tip: when something is working, lever it up. Push it. Put your foot on the gas. The second is a life lesson from my father, who spent his career running Boy Scout councils and never traded a stock. Plan in reverse. Write the goal on the calendar first. Work backwards.
</p>

I promise you these are the same idea, and together they are most of the reason I have been able to stay in this business as long as I have.

## The disposition effect, or why you hate your winners

Let me start with the trading part. There is a stock I've been trading — CTRP, as a recent example, though it does not have to be that one — and it has worked for me almost every day. It gaps up, it hangs, it fills. I take the short. I make money. And every single morning, there is a voice in my head saying, *you should probably stop. This cannot keep working. Something bad is about to happen.*

If I listen to that voice, I leave tens of thousands of dollars on the table over the course of a year.

That voice has a name. Academics call it the *disposition effect*, which is not a great name but fine. Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman, building on Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, described it as the very well-documented tendency for retail traders to sell their winners too early and hold their losers too long. Terrance Odean's 1998 study of real retail brokerage accounts found that traders sold winning positions at roughly 1.7 times the rate they sold losers — and the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept by about 3.4 percentage points over the following year.

That is not rounding error. That is a career.

## Why we do it, briefly, with apologies

The reason is not mysterious. A winning trade feels psychologically fragile. You have profit on the screen, and every tick against you now *takes something away*. Loss aversion — the finding, by now established in too many experiments to count, that losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains — means the fear of giving back the gain is louder in your head than the excitement of extending it.

So you close. You congratulate yourself on being disciplined. And you leave the compound return to somebody else.

The traders in Schwager's *Market Wizards* books say this, collectively, in about a hundred different ways. Ed Seykota: the trend is your friend, except at the end when it bends. Jesse Livermore: it was never my thinking that made the big money for me, it was always my sitting. Paul Tudor Jones built an entire career on riding the occasional monster trade while keeping losses small. Minervini and O'Neil both pyramid *into* positions that are working — they add on strength, not weakness.

This is the part most retail traders get exactly backwards. They average down on losers (because it feels like a deal) and scale out of winners (because it feels safe). The research says the professionals do the opposite, and they do it deliberately.

## How to press without blowing up

Now, here is the thing I do not want you to hear, because this argument has a wrong version and a right version and the wrong version is very expensive.

The wrong version is: "go bet the farm on your favorite setup."

The right version is: when you find something that is working, *and you have already defined your maximum loss*, you should not be afraid to size up.

That second clause is doing all the work.

<Pullquote>
Without the defined loss, "press your edge" becomes "blow up your account." With it, pressing your edge is just arithmetic.
</Pullquote>

The single most important question you can answer before you enter any trade — any trade, but especially a sized-up one — is this: *how far will I let this go if I am wrong?* Not "I'll figure it out." Not "I'll be careful." A number. A price. A line on the chart. Van Tharp built his whole methodology around this, and for good reason. You define your 1R risk. You size the position so that 1R is a tolerable dollar loss. And then the fact that sometimes a trade works for 5R or 10R is what actually pays you. The asymmetry pays you, not the cleverness.

Without that line, pressing an edge collapses into leverage theater, which is what happens when a trader has conviction without proof and access to margin.

## My dad's calendar

Which brings me to my dad.

My father, Bob Booker, ran Boy Scout councils in North Carolina, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Miami, Orange County, and finally the San Gabriel Valley for seventeen years. He raised millions of dollars for programs no other council was running. He did this before email, before smartphones, before any of the tools that supposedly make modern work easier.

He did it with a calendar.

At the start of every year, my dad would sit down — first alone, then with his team — and he would write the biggest goals of the year into specific dates. Not aspirations. *Dates.* By March 15, we will have raised two million dollars. By September 12, we will host this event. And then — this is the part I eventually stole for trading — he would work *backwards* from those dates. What has to be true two weeks before the March 15 target? What has to be true two months before? Who do I need to call, and when?

He built his own day planner because Franklin Covey was not specific enough for him, which is a sentence I can still hear him saying. He used to tell me: *values are good, values are important, but I cannot write "be honest" in my calendar. I have to write the number I am raising, and the date, and the name of the person I am calling to ask for it.*

## The research, briefly and with some awe

The academic literature validates my dad's approach almost embarrassingly well. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's goal-setting research, developed across hundreds of studies over fifty years, shows that specific and difficult goals produce dramatically better performance than vague "do your best" goals. Peter Gollwitzer's work on *implementation intentions* — the "if X, then Y" format — shows that people who specify when, where, and how they will act are roughly two to three times more likely to follow through than people who just state the goal.

My dad's reverse calendar was an implementation intention factory. He was not writing "raise more money." He was writing "on April 3, call Jim about the auction."

He did not know the research. He also did not need it. The research caught up.

## The translation to trading

For a trader, the translation is direct, and it is the missing piece that makes "press your edge when it is working" into something actionable rather than vague.

Do not write "I want to be profitable this year."

Write: by December 31, my account will be at $X. Which means by September 30 it needs to be at $Y. Which means between now and then I need to average $Z per week. Which means I need to be taking roughly N trades per week at my current win rate and R multiple.

Now you have a plan with edges you can push against. Now "pressing your edge when it is working" has context. You know whether you are ahead of pace or behind. You know whether today is a day to size up or a day to protect what you have. The calendar tells you. The number tells you. You are not improvising.

Improvisation, as I keep saying in these essays, is mostly what breaks traders. Structure is what keeps them in business. The reverse calendar is structure.

## The part I almost left out

Here is the part of the story I almost left out, because it does not sound like it belongs in a trading essay. But it is the whole thing, so I am putting it in.

My dad ran those councils and raised those millions, and if I called his office — back when you had to call an office, and a human had to find him — somebody would go track him down, pull him out of whatever meeting he was in, and put him on the phone with me. Every single time. I do not remember one instance where my dad was too busy to take the call.

I think about this a lot when I watch myself start to compound. When a trade is working, when a business is working, when the calendar is filling up with the right kind of wins. The temptation is to let the pursuit eat everything else.

What my dad taught me, without ever saying it out loud, is that the goal only counts if you are still the person you were when you set it. The reverse calendar is for the work. The phone call is for the life. If you compound one by burning the other, you have not actually won anything. You have built a number that cannot be handed to anybody.

## The summary, because my producer asked

Press your edge when it is working. Define your maximum loss first. Write the goal on the calendar in ink, then work backwards in pencil. And when your kid climbs in bed with you while you are setting up a trade on the Australian dollar — close the laptop.
