<p className="lede">
One of the strangest habits traders have is that they spend years looking for something that works and then, once they find it, they become oddly hesitant about doing more of it.
</p>

This is the mistake I want to talk about, because I make it and I watch other people make it and I suspect you have made it. If something is working and it keeps working, the job is to press it — carefully, repeatedly, with risk still under control — until it stops working.

That sounds obvious. Almost no one actually does it.

## The emotional shape of most traders

Most traders are built for discovery, not exploitation. They love the hunt more than the harvest. They like finding a new pattern, a new market, a new explanation, a new favorite ticker. Repetition feels less glamorous than discovery, even though — and this is the inconvenient part — repetition is usually where the money is.

I am not saying bet recklessly on whatever worked yesterday. That is the other mistake, and it is also common, and we'll get to it. I am saying that once you have something demonstrably repeatable, the common amateur move is to take your foot off the gas too early, out of a kind of polite nervousness that it might not work again.

Your nervous system doesn't really trust the thing that's working. Which is fair. You should probably listen to your nervous system about a lot of things. But not this one.

## Two stories, one point

Let me give you two versions of the mistake, one small and one large.

The small one is a stock I've watched for a long time — `CTRP`, as an example, though there are others — that reliably drops after gapping up at the open. Not every time. Often enough. Recognizable setup. Understandable risk. Doing it again isn't an act of faith; it's an act of arithmetic. And still, for years, I would take the trade once, make the money, and then find some reason not to take the next one.

The larger one is longer-memory. Think about a real-estate downturn you remember well, or the old British pound/yen carry trade from 2005 to 2007. The opportunity was obvious enough that the real regret, in retrospect, was not having done more of the same thing while conditions lasted. People often knew, at least roughly, what was happening. What they failed to do was organize themselves around repetition before the window closed.

Both stories have the same shape. The edge was there. The mind wandered away from it.

## Depth, not breadth

There is a useful correction in this somewhere, and it is this: trading culture overemphasizes diversification of attention. A lot of profitable traders are not succeeding because they know more things. They are succeeding because they know one or two things *deeply enough to repeat them without drama.*

That distinction matters.

A repeatable edge is not a setup that worked once. It is something that appears under recognizable conditions, with understandable risk, often enough that doing it again is not an act of faith. It is, at that point, closer to a business than a hunch.

And this is where many traders quietly sabotage themselves. They find the edge, but then they get nervous about leaning into it. Or — and this is the other mistake, the mirror image — they size it too aggressively, too quickly, without preserving the conditions that made the edge tolerable in the first place.

<Pullquote>
The right move is not maximal aggression. The right move is controlled scaling.
</Pullquote>

## Controlled scaling, briefly, with apologies

This idea shows up in serious performance domains of every kind. Good operators do not merely identify what works. They build a way to do more of it without destroying the system. In business, this means adding capacity without blowing up quality. In sports, it means increasing load without losing form. In trading, it means increasing size while keeping loss bounded and process intact.

Said plainly: there is no problem gradually increasing trade size and committing more capital to something that keeps working, so long as you are still controlling how much you lose if it goes wrong.

That "so long as" is everything. Without it, the whole idea collapses into leverage theater, which is what happens when someone has conviction without proof and access to margin.

Kelly's 1956 paper on how to size bets is the canonical math here, and I will not pretend to do it justice. The short version, which I will summarize briefly and probably badly, is that the correct size is a function of the *true* edge, not the edge you hope you have, and that overestimating the edge hurts you more than underestimating it. Ed Thorp's memoir is the accessible version of the same argument.

## Conviction is not proof

One reason traders screw this up is that they confuse conviction with proof.

If a setup has worked a few times, the ego wants to jump directly from "this may be real" to "this is now my identity." That usually leads to one of two bad outcomes — timidity, where the trader is so worried about being wrong that they take the edge in tiny dribbles forever, or recklessness, where they take the edge in one enormous leveraged bet before they've earned the right to.

Neither is especially useful.

A better response is, I am sorry to report, boring. Measure it. Repeat it. Increase thoughtfully. Let the size rise more slowly than the ego wants. The ego wants the whole thing now. The ego is not operating the account; you are.

## Show up at the same time

The Mexican peso thing, the Australian dollar thing — these are not presented, in my experience, as mystical once-in-a-lifetime revelations. They are presented as things that happen in a *time window*, often enough to be worth showing up for.

That is an underrated criterion.

A tradable idea should not merely be theoretically profitable. It should be practically repeatable. If you can show up at roughly the same time, under roughly the same conditions, with roughly the same plan, and keep getting a similar opportunity — you have something closer to a business than a hunch.

Academics call this, in a loosely related context, *implementation intentions* — the finding that people execute goals better when they attach them to specific conditions. "If X happens, then I do Y." Repeatable trading windows are psychologically powerful for exactly that reason. They reduce choice overload. They make execution less dependent on mood. They move the trader from broad ambition toward scheduled behavior.

And scheduled behavior is where repeatability becomes real.

## Nothing works forever, which is the point

One more wrinkle. "Do more of what works" does not mean "believe it will work forever." Nothing works forever. A trader does not need permanence to justify repetition. They only need a live edge and a process ready to exploit it while it remains valid.

Traders often think acknowledging impermanence requires undercommitting. The opposite is true. Awareness that a pattern will eventually stop is *exactly why* you press it while it's still there. The carry trade from 2005 to 2007 was one of the few times I really put my foot on the gas, and the reason I was willing to was not that I thought it would last forever. The reason I was willing to was that I could see it working now, and "now" was the only relevant time horizon.

The edge does not mind that you know it will stop. You minding is the problem.

## The double mistake

When traders get cute, they usually leave money on the table twice. First by taking too long to trust what is already working. Then by wandering away from it before the edge has actually deteriorated.

The market does not require endless novelty. Quite often, it requires the maturity to keep pressing the same useful button.

Find the thing. Confirm it's repeatable. Tie it to conditions you can show up for. Increase size gradually with loss control intact. Keep pressing until it stops.
