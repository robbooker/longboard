<p className="lede">
Look, one of the stranger habits traders have is that they spend years looking for something that works, and then, when they find it, they get weirdly shy about doing more of it.
</p>

That's the mistake I want to write about, because I make it, and I watch other people make it, and I suspect, if you have been doing this for any length of time, that you have made it too. If a thing is working and continues to work, the job — the actual job, not the one the ego would prefer — is to press it. Carefully. Repeatedly. With the downside still bounded. Until it stops.

This sounds obvious. Basically nobody does it.

## The emotional shape of most traders

Most traders are built for *discovery*, not *exploitation*. They love the hunt more than the harvest. They like finding a new pattern, a new market, a new explanation, a new favorite ticker. Repetition feels less glamorous than discovery, even though — and this is the unflattering part — repetition is where the money is.

I am not saying bet recklessly on whatever worked yesterday. That's the other mistake, and it's also popular, and we'll get to it. I am saying that once you have a thing that is demonstrably repeatable, the ordinary amateur move is to take your foot off the gas too early, out of a kind of polite nervousness that the thing might not work again.

Which, fine, it might not. But the polite nervousness is not a strategy. The polite nervousness is the reason you underperform your own edge.

## Two stories, one shape

Let me give you two versions of the mistake. One small, one large.

The small version: there is a stock I've watched for a long time — let's call it CTRP, though there are others — that reliably drops after gapping up at the open. Not every time. Often enough. Recognizable setup, understandable risk. Doing it again is not, in any serious sense, an act of faith; it is closer to arithmetic. And still, for years, I would take the trade once, make the money, and then find some reason not to take the next one. I'd find a reason. There is always a reason. Reasons are free.

The large version has a longer memory attached to it. Think about a real-estate downturn you remember personally, or the British pound / yen carry trade from roughly 2005 to 2007. The opportunity was obvious enough that the actual regret — the one that sticks — was not having done *more* of the same thing while the conditions lasted. People often knew, at least roughly, what was happening. What they failed to do was organize themselves around repetition before the window closed.

Both stories are the same story. The edge was right there. The mind wandered off.

## Depth is the unfashionable secret

There's a quiet correction in this that almost nobody wants to hear, and it is that trading culture overemphasizes diversification of attention.

A lot of profitable traders are not succeeding because they know *more* things. They are succeeding because they know one or two things *deeply enough to repeat them without drama.*

That is a different claim than the usual one, and the difference matters.

A repeatable edge is not a setup that worked once. It is something that appears under recognizable conditions, with understandable risk, often enough that doing it again is not faith but procedure. At that point it is, for all practical purposes, closer to a business than a hunch.

And this is where a lot of traders quietly sabotage themselves. They find the edge — actually find it — and then get nervous about leaning into it. Or, the mirror image, they size it too aggressively too quickly, without preserving the conditions that made the edge tolerable in the first place.

<Pullquote>
The right move is not maximal aggression. The right move is controlled scaling. Which sounds like a phrase out of a pamphlet, and is, and is also correct.
</Pullquote>

## Controlled scaling, briefly, with apologies

This idea shows up in serious performance domains of every kind. Good operators do not merely identify what works; they build a way to do more of it without destroying the system. In business, that's adding capacity without blowing up quality. In sports, it's increasing load without losing form. In trading, it's increasing size while keeping loss bounded and process intact.

Said plainly: there is no problem with gradually increasing your trade size and committing more capital to something that keeps working, so long as you are still controlling how much you lose if it goes wrong.

That "so long as" is, I'm sorry, the entire argument.

Without it, the whole idea collapses into leverage theater, which is the thing that happens when a trader has conviction without proof and access to margin.

The math here has a name. John Kelly's 1956 paper on bet sizing is the canonical reference, and I will summarize it very briefly and probably badly, because it is, on top of everything else, a paper about information theory that ended up having opinions about blackjack. The short version is that the right size of a bet is a function of the *true* edge, not the hoped-for edge, and that overestimating your edge hurts you worse than underestimating it. Ed Thorp's memoir, which is also about blackjack before it is about markets, is the accessible version of the same claim. You can read Thorp in a weekend. You cannot read Kelly in a weekend. This is fine.

## Conviction is not proof, which is annoying

One of the reasons traders make this specific mess is that they confuse conviction with proof.

If a setup has worked a few times, the ego very much wants to jump directly from "this may be real" to "this is now my identity." And this leads to one of two equally bad places. Timidity — the trader is so worried about being wrong that they take the edge in microscopic dribbles forever, never really participating in the thing they found. Or recklessness — they take the edge in one enormous leveraged bet before they've earned the right to, and the first drawdown sends the whole enterprise backwards.

Neither of these is useful.

A better response is — I am sorry to report this — boring. Measure it. Repeat it. Increase the size gradually. Let the size rise more slowly than the ego wants it to.

The ego wants the whole thing now. The ego is not operating the account. You are.

## Show up at the same time

There is one more piece, and it is the cleanest heuristic I have for whether something is actually an edge versus a story about an edge.

A tradable idea should not merely be *theoretically* profitable. It should be *practically repeatable.* If you can show up at roughly the same time, under roughly the same conditions, with roughly the same plan, and keep getting a similar opportunity — you have something closer to a business than a hunch.

The examples I keep coming back to, the peso and the Aussie dollar windows that I've referenced in old episodes, are not mystical once-in-a-lifetime revelations. They are things that happened in a *time window*, on a calendar, often enough to be worth showing up for.

That is an underrated criterion. It is underrated because it sounds small. It is also the criterion that separates traders who eventually have a thing they can do from traders who keep half-discovering things and then misplacing them.

Academics call this, in an adjacent context, *implementation intentions* — Peter Gollwitzer's finding that people execute goals much better when they attach them to specific conditions. "If X happens, then I do Y." Repeatable trading windows are psychologically powerful for exactly this reason. They reduce choice overload. They make execution less dependent on mood. They convert vague ambition into a schedule, which is the only form of ambition that actually works.

And scheduled behavior is where repeatability becomes real.

## Nothing works forever, which is — yes — the point

Here is the last wrinkle, and it is the one that gets people wrong-footed.

"Do more of what is working" does not mean "believe it will work forever." Nothing works forever. You do not need permanence to justify repetition. You only need a live edge and a process ready to exploit it while it remains valid.

Traders often get this backwards and think acknowledging impermanence requires undercommitting. The opposite is true. The awareness that a pattern will eventually stop is *precisely why* you press it while it is still there. The carry trade from 2005 to 2007 was one of the few times in my career I really put my foot on the gas, and the reason I was willing to was not that I thought it would last forever. The reason I was willing to was that I could see it working *now*, and "now" was the only relevant time horizon on the table.

The edge does not mind that you know it will stop. *You* minding is the problem.

## The double mistake, which is one mistake wearing two hats

When traders get cute, they leave money on the table twice. First, by taking too long to trust what is already working. Second, by wandering away from it before the edge has actually deteriorated.

The market, in my experience, does not require endless novelty. Most of the time, it requires the much less interesting virtue of maturity — the willingness to keep pressing the same useful button, with the same bounded risk, under the same conditions, for as long as the button keeps working.

That is almost all of it. The rest is details.
