The Psychology
of Trading

Shame, regret, and the discipline to stay in the game.

Rob Booker

Open quiet. No hype. Tonight is not about the system — not one chart. We're going to talk about the thing that actually decides whether you make it: what happens inside you when a trade goes bad. Set the contract with the room: I'm going to go first on every hard thing I ask of you tonight.
The Story

The $165,000 walk.

On a walk with Igor.
The stock at $66 a share.
A chance to close it.
I didn't.
–$165,000
The worse the loss got,
the worse I got at closing it.
It was the money we had set aside for a house.
02
Tell the whole story straight. The walk. The stock at $66. The chance to close it and the decision not to. The phone call: "I just lost all of the money we were going to put toward a house." It was cash, set aside. Not financial ruin — but it was the house we'd been looking at. Then her answer: "I've made plenty of mistakes, and you stuck with me." She didn't hand me shame. She handed me a choice: keep feeling bad, or change. Don't draw the lesson yet. Let the story sit. Everything tonight comes back to this.
What Pressure Does

The worse it gets, the worse we act.

HidingYou stop looking at the trade. You hide from it inside your own head.
FreezingYou know what the plan says. You can't make your hand do it.
“It always comes back”The most expensive sentence in trading.
Build the guardrails before the crisis — not during it.
03
In the moment a trade runs past the stop, nobody is thinking rationally. You're hiding from the trade. You're choking. You're frozen. Or worse — telling yourself it always comes back. If we haven't learned to avoid situations where the worse it gets, the worse we act — we haven't learned anything. The fix is never made during the crisis. It's made before: size, max loss, rules, the people you tell.
Managing the Bad Trade

Close is close enough.

–$160
what I took on a trade I'd already judged badly
thousands
what my max loss allowed me to lose instead
A small loss on a trade that's already gone bad is a gift. You don't need every trade to become a win.
04
Why did I take the loss? Because I wanted to be done. By 4:30 Eastern I'm tired and I want to close out my trading. And because I'd already decided I didn't do a good job on the trade — got in early, was concerned. Ask the room: ever been in a bad trade, it gets close to break-even, and you don't take it because you want a little profit — and then it gets worse again? Once you've decided the trade was bad, close is close enough. It feels really good to manage a trade well and shut it down near your entry. Losing $160 when the real max loss is in the thousands — that's a gift.
The First Demon

Shame destroys traders.

One bad loss after sixty good days — and the spiral starts. I've watched traders take months off. A year off. Never come back. It was never the loss. It was the shame.

Feeling bad about a lossNormal. Useful. It passes.
Shame about who you arePoison. It compounds. It ends careers.
05
Ask it directly: have you ever felt shame about a loss? I have. We can make money 60 days in a row, know ourselves to be good traders — then one mistake, one loss we let run, maybe the broker has to close it for us — and the spiral starts. Watch the self-talk: "I made the same mistake, I made the same mistake." That's victimization. That's luxuriating in the disappointment. It's dark and it's bad. The recovery process is always the same: reduce size and go right back at it. Not to win it all back — to prove to yourself you can still trade. Need two or three months off? Fine. But come back.
The Required Mindset
You cannot trade well while believing you are bad, broken, or undeserving.

Pathologically confident. Never arrogant. Solid in yourself — especially on the days you were wrong.

06
To be a good trader — short or long — you have to be almost pathologically confident in yourself without being arrogant. You cannot feel like a bad person. You cannot feel like you don't deserve the wins. Confidence isn't the belief you'll never be wrong. It's the ability to be wrong, pay for it, and still be solid in yourself an hour later.
The Second Demon

Regret is not a trading plan.

“I could have held it.”
“I should have held it.”
“I knew it would go to that level.”
“Next time I'm going to…”
Stop it.

You will never refine yourself into perfect.
Good enough is amazing.

07
Regret is looking at a trade after you closed it for a $165 loss and saying "I could have held it. I should have held it. I knew it." Say your piece. Say whatever you need to say. Then stop acting like you can refine things to the point where you become perfect at this. There is no perfect. Do good enough. Good enough — done every day, without drama — is amazing.
The Third Demon — Me First

That is who you are.
Reduce your trade size.

If you let even one trade run past your stop in the last 90 days — sized big enough to blow past your max loss — whether or not it turned out okay:

You are going to do it again.
So size down to where doing it again can't take you out of the game. Don't stop trading. Protect the money.
08
I'm going to raise my hand first. You don't have to raise yours. I have let trades go too far against me — even after I promised I wouldn't. You're not going to change overnight. There's no awakening where you say "never again" and mean it. You'll do it again. That's who you are. That's who I am. So reduce your trade size. And to the person thinking "but I have to trade big enough to support my family": how are you going to support your family with no trading account? I'd rather miss some bills and grow into a trader who makes millions than go 50 weeks pretending everything's fine and get punched in the mouth. I reduced my size today — and made a whole bunch of money doing it. How is that even possible? It is.
When Someone Blows Up

They are the same as you.

Not worse than you. Not dumber than you.
A normal person, under pressure, hiding from a trade.
They just took a trade you didn't take.
Next time around, it could be your turn. Learn from their experience — it's the cheapest tuition you'll ever get.
09
When someone blows up an account in our group, we have to learn from it — without looking down on them. In the moment they took that trade and let it run past their stop, they were not thinking rationally. Hiding. Choking. Frozen. Or thinking "it always comes back." In that moment they're a normal person. No different from you. Next time around, it could be your turn. You can make tens of thousands of dollars a day shorting stocks. The only thing that prevents it is ruining everything by losing all your money.
The Hard Truth

The system is not the problem.

“It was the Chinese stocks.”
“It was the setup.”
“It was the scanner.”

Nobody loses money because they traded a Chinese stock. You lost money because you didn't close the trade.

If the system were enough to make everyone rich, everyone in this room would already be rich.
10
The system has not changed very much. I told Cameron — one of the original 17 from Houston three years ago — "the system hasn't changed, man. It's time to come back." If you haven't made the money you wanted short selling, it has absolutely nothing to do with your technicals. If you think you'll be fine because you avoid Chinese stocks or some other category — you're focused on the system. That's not why anyone lost money. The system isn't perfect. It might not even be very good. It's the greatest system I've ever traded or taught — and it is still not the key. We are. We have to do a better job of becoming better people. There's no way around it.
Honesty Creates Freedom

Stop saying sorry. Change.

An apology repeated is a behavior unchanged. Coming clean — about not closing losers, about not being rational — is what sets you free to fix the conditions.

“I'm in a bad trade.”
“I'm not doing well today.”
Say it out loud. To yourself. To us. That's what this group is for.
11
Have you ever told someone: stop telling me sorry and just change? That occurred to me after the big loss. I didn't want to keep apologizing for the same thing and not change. The mistake was horrible — it cost us the house we'd been looking at. But it freed me up to be more honest. I had to say: I am the kind of person who lets trades run, so I can never put myself in a position to trade above a certain size. A max loss with your broker is good — but not complete, because you can always turn it off. You can be in a bad trade and call your broker. The better thing is to stay grounded — and be honest with each other in real time: "I'm in the middle of a trade and it's not going well." We have to be able to say that to each other.
