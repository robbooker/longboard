# Communication Standards
## Radical Clarity, Honest Feedback, and High Ownership

**Company:** Driftstone Holdings, LLC  
**Status:** Working internal standard

## 1. Purpose

We want a company where people say what they actually think, surface problems early, ask for clarity when something is unclear, and take ownership for moving work forward.

The goal is not to make communication cold, harsh, or robotic. The goal is to remove fear, vagueness, hidden disagreement, unnecessary politeness rituals, and emotional interpretation that slow down good work.

We can be warm, funny, caring, and human while also being extremely clear.

This standard is inspired in part by Bridgewater Associates' public ideas around truth, transparency, idea meritocracy, learning from mistakes, and challenging ideas regardless of hierarchy. We are adapting those ideas for a smaller, fast-moving company. We are not trying to copy Bridgewater's culture wholesale.

> **Clarity is kind. Truth improves the work. Directness should serve the goal, not the ego.**

## 2. Core Principles

Everyone at Driftstone is expected to:

1. Say what you actually think.
2. Surface problems early.
3. Separate facts from interpretation.
4. Give a recommendation whenever you reasonably can.
5. Ask for clarity when clarity is needed to execute correctly.
6. Challenge ideas regardless of who proposed them.
7. Own mistakes quickly and learn from them.
8. Receive correction as information about the work, not as a verdict on your worth.
9. Close loops. Do not confuse activity with completion.
10. Once a decision is made, execute it clearly and decisively.

## 3. Truth Over Comfort, Without Cruelty

If something is wrong, say it. If a result looks bad, say it. If you disagree, say it. If you made a mistake, say it. If you do not understand something, say it. If you think there is a better way, say it.

Direct communication should be specific, accurate, timely, focused on the work, useful to the recipient, and connected to an outcome or next step.

Directness is never permission for personal attacks, humiliation, aggression for its own sake, vague criticism, emotional dumping, or cruelty disguised as honesty.

The standard is not nice versus mean. The standard is:

> **Is this true, useful, clear, and in service of the goal?**

## 4. Corrections Are About the Work

A correction means something in the work needs to change. It does not mean the person is bad, unintelligent, unwanted, or failing as a human being.

When giving a correction, focus on:
1. What happened.
2. What is wrong or missing.
3. Why it matters.
4. What needs to happen next.

When receiving a correction, first understand it. Good responses include:
- "Got it."
- "I understand what needs to change."
- "I missed that. I'll fix it."
- "I understand the issue, but I disagree with the proposed solution. Here's why."
- "I need one point clarified before I execute."

Avoid immediately defending intent, excessively apologizing, attacking yourself, or going silent because the feedback is uncomfortable.

## 5. Ask for Clarity — Do Not Pretend to Understand

Questions are encouraged when they improve execution. We would rather answer one clear question now than clean up an avoidable mistake later.

Asking for clarity is not weakness, resistance, or incompetence. But questions should not become a substitute for judgment, ownership, or decision-making.

**Good:**  
> "Before I move forward, clarify one thing: are we sending this today or tomorrow?"

**Less useful:**  
> "I'm sorry, I may be overthinking this and maybe I misunderstood, but I just wanted to make sure..."

> **If you need clarity, ask the smallest question that will unlock execution.**

## 6. Speak Up Regardless of Hierarchy

The best idea should not lose because the person who had it had less authority.

Employees are expected to challenge ideas, including ideas from founders and executives, when they see a meaningful risk, factual error, better approach, missed opportunity, conflict with the stated goal, or numbers that do not make sense.

**Good disagreement:**  
> "I disagree with this approach because our current numbers suggest the problem is the landing page, not traffic. I recommend we fix conversion before increasing ad spend."

Power should not replace reasoning. The person with authority still owns the final decision. Once a decision is made, the team should execute unless meaningful new information changes the situation.

## 7. Facts → Interpretation → Recommendation → Action

Whenever possible, communicate in this order:

**Fact:** What actually happened?  
**Interpretation:** What do you think it means?  
**Recommendation:** What do you think we should do?  
**Action:** Who does what next, and by when?

Example:

> **Fact:** We spent $1,800 and generated 620 landing-page visits, but only 31 registrations.  
> **Interpretation:** The ads are getting attention, but we are losing people after the click.  
> **Recommendation:** Test the landing page before increasing spend.  
> **Action:** I will draft two headline/form versions today and have them ready by 3 PM.

Data without interpretation often creates more work for the person receiving it.

## 8. Recommendations Over Permission-Seeking

When you reasonably know what should happen, recommend or act.

Prefer:
- "Send the email at 2 PM."
- "Move the webinar to Thursday so we have another day to promote it."
- "I'm going to reconcile these deposits before I send the report."

Instead of:
- "Would it maybe make sense to send the email at 2 PM?"
- "I was thinking maybe Thursday might be better, but whatever you think."
- "Do you want me to reconcile these first?"

The goal is not to eliminate genuine questions. The goal is to distinguish between:
- **I need information to proceed**
- **I already have enough information, but I am afraid to make the call**

## 9. Mistakes Are Expected. Failure to Learn Is Not.

Reasonable mistakes are part of fast-moving work.

When you make a mistake:
1. Say what happened.
2. Take ownership.
3. Correct it.
4. Identify why it happened.
5. Change the process if needed.
6. Move forward.

**Good:**  
> "I sent the wrong link. That's on me. I corrected the email, verified the new link end-to-end, and added a final-link check to the send checklist."

The lesson is more valuable than the shame.

## 10. High Ownership Means Closing the Loop

Starting work is not the same as finishing work. Research is not an outcome. Creating an asset is not necessarily an outcome. "Working on it" is not a status.

High ownership means knowing what success looks like and staying with the work until the loop is closed.

**Weak:**  
> "I worked on the ads and did some research today."

**Strong:**  
> "Meta is live. Google was rejected for misleading-content language, so I rewrote the copy and resubmitted it. Meta is approved; Google is pending. I'll check again at 3 PM."

We care about:

> **Work → Result → Meaning → Next Action**

## 11. Status Updates Should Reduce Questions

A good status update should leave the recipient knowing:
- Where things stand
- What changed
- What is blocked
- What matters
- What happens next
- Whether they need to do anything

Recommended format:

**Status:** What is true now?  
**Meaning:** What should we understand from it?  
**Next:** What are you doing next?  
**Need:** Is anything required from someone else?

## 12. Give Instructions That Can Be Executed

An instruction should answer, when relevant:
- What needs to happen?
- Who owns it?
- When?
- What does done look like?
- What dependency matters?

**Better:**  
> "Record the 30-second retargeting video tomorrow after the 10 AM webinar. Upload it to Drive and send the link by noon."

**Weaker:**  
> "When you get a chance, we probably need that video soon."

If the instruction belongs on a calendar, put it on the calendar. If it belongs in a task system, create the task.

## 13. Prioritize When Urgency Is High

When someone is overloaded, do not send five equally urgent demands. Prioritize.

Example:

> "Priority order:
> 1. Record webinar video.
> 2. Approve email.
> 3. Review analytics report."

Clarity includes priority.

## 14. Separate Urgency From Emotion

Urgent does not have to mean angry. Direct does not have to mean upset.

> "Stop work on the other items and fix this now. The registration link is broken and emails are already going out."

That message is urgent because the situation requires it. It does not require personal blame.

Likewise, a recipient should not infer anger merely because a message is short.

## 15. Be Human

We do not want sterile corporate language.

Warmth is allowed. Humor is allowed. Emojis are allowed when appropriate. Personality is allowed.

The standard is not "sound formal."

> **Be human without making the message harder to understand.**

## 16. Decision Rules

**Before a decision:** Debate openly.  
**During a decision:** Bring the best facts, reasoning, risks, and alternatives.  
**After a decision:** Execute.  
**Reopen a decision:** When meaningful new information appears, not simply because someone still dislikes the outcome.

## 17. Common Anti-Patterns

### Apology Sandwich
"I'm so sorry and I know you're busy and I hate to bother you, but..."

Replace it with the actual message.

### Activity Dump
"Today I researched competitors, worked on ads, watched videos, wrote notes..."

Say what changed and what happens next.

### Hidden Disagreement
"Sounds good!" when you believe the plan will fail.

Speak up.

### Vague Problem
"Something seems weird with Google."

Say what looks wrong and why.

### Criticism Without a Recommendation
"The landing page isn't good."

When possible:
> "The page is losing people between click and registration. I recommend testing a shorter page and simpler form."

### Personalization Spiral
"They corrected my work, therefore they are disappointed in me, therefore I am failing."

Stop at the first fact:
> "They corrected the work."

Understand it, improve it, continue.

### Certainty Bluff
Do not pretend to know something you do not know.

> "I don't know yet. My current hypothesis is X, and I'm checking Y to verify it."

## 18. Before → After Examples

### Overly soft → Direct

**Before:**  
"I was thinking maybe if you have some time tomorrow after the webinar it might be good to record that video..."

**After:**  
"Record the campaign video tomorrow after the webinar. We need it for retargeting."

### Sharp → Direct and productive

**Before:**  
"This isn't what I asked for. We can't keep doing this. Fix it."

**After:**  
"This isn't what I asked for. Fix this version before moving on, and make sure it matches the requirements we agreed on."

### Vague status → Outcome-focused

**Before:**  
"I worked on Google Ads most of the day."

**After:**  
"Google rejected the campaign for misleading-content language. I rewrote the copy and resubmitted it. Approval is pending, and I'll check again this afternoon."

### Admitting a mistake

**Before:**  
"I'm so sorry. I feel awful. I should have caught this."

**After:**  
"I missed the incorrect link. I fixed it, tested the full registration flow, and added a final-link check before future sends."

### Asking for clarity

**Before:**  
"Sorry, I may have misunderstood, but I wanted to check because I don't want to mess this up..."

**After:**  
"Clarify one thing before I move forward: should this go out today or tomorrow?"

## 19. One-Page Standard

- **Say the thing.** Do not hide important information behind politeness, fear, or hierarchy.
- **Be accurate.** Separate what happened from what you think it means.
- **Bring a recommendation.** Do not simply transfer a problem when you can help solve it.
- **Ask for clarity.** If one question will prevent a mistake, ask it clearly.
- **Challenge ideas, not people.** The best reasoning should win regardless of title.
- **Corrections are about the work.** Receive feedback as information you can use.
- **Own mistakes.** Say it, fix it, learn from it, improve the system.
- **Close the loop.** Activity is not completion.
- **Make the next action obvious.** Who does what, and by when?
- **Be human.** Warmth and humor are welcome. Confusion is not.

# AI Communication Standard

## 20. Primary Goal

The AI is not a "make everything nicer" tool.

It should make communication clearer, more direct, more actionable, easier to understand, and more aligned with these standards while preserving substantive meaning.

## 21. The AI Must Preserve

The AI must preserve:
- Facts
- Decisions
- Dollar amounts
- Dates
- Times
- Deadlines
- Names
- Ownership
- Level of urgency
- Material criticism
- Material disagreement
- Requests for clarification
- Commitments
- Conditions
- Risks
- Uncertainty when uncertainty exists

If the source says "I disagree," the output may not convert it into agreement. If the source says "do this today," the output may not weaken it. If the source is uncertain, the output may not invent certainty.

## 22. The AI May Change

The AI may:
- Remove repetition
- Remove unnecessary apologies
- Remove unnecessary permission-seeking
- Shorten background that does not change the instruction
- Reorder information for clarity
- Separate multiple requests into a sequence
- Clarify the next action
- Remove unnecessary emotional sharpness
- Preserve warmth while reducing clutter
- Identify the underlying intent
- Distinguish fact, interpretation, recommendation, and action

## 23. The AI Must Never

The AI must never:
- Invent facts, deadlines, commitments, or instructions
- Change dollar amounts, dates, or times
- Hide real disagreement
- Convert criticism into fake praise
- Add reassurance or emotional claims the sender did not make
- Change the true level of urgency
- Turn a question into a decision
- Turn a recommendation into an order unless clearly authorized
- Remove a legitimate request for clarity
- Infer someone's emotional state as fact
- Use tone smoothing to conceal a real execution issue

When ambiguity is material, preserve it or flag it rather than guessing.

## 24. Recommended AI Output

Each transformation should support:

**Original** — exact raw message  
**Intent** — one sentence describing what the sender is trying to accomplish  
**Suggested Message** — rewritten version  
**Why It Changed** — optional short explanation of the standard applied

The sender must be able to edit, regenerate, copy, approve, and view the original.

**Nothing should auto-send.**

# Private DM Profile: Liz ↔ Rob

## 25. Liz → Rob

### Objective
Preserve Liz's warmth and reasoning while making the action or decision immediately obvious.

### Transformation Rules
- Find the actual request, recommendation, or decision.
- Put it early.
- Remove repetition.
- Remove unnecessary apology language.
- Replace unnecessary permission-seeking with a recommendation or instruction.
- Keep the minimum context Rob needs to act.
- Make dates/times explicit when provided.
- Prefer one clear item at a time when practical.
- Preserve genuine warmth and humor when useful.
- Preserve legitimate uncertainty.
- Preserve requests for clarity when information is genuinely missing.

**Example**

Original:
> "I was thinking maybe once you're done with the webinar and if you have time today we could possibly have you look at the email because I'd really love to get that sent before tonight if possible."

Intent:
> Rob needs to review the email today so it can be sent tonight.

Suggested:
> "Review the email after the webinar today so we can send it tonight."

## 26. Rob → Liz

### Objective
Preserve Rob's decision, urgency, correction, and directness while making the message maximally clear and productive.

### Transformation Rules
- Preserve the instruction exactly in substance.
- Preserve legitimate criticism and urgency.
- Organize multiple instructions.
- Remove unnecessary personal sharpness if present.
- Keep the focus on the work, problem, and next action.
- Do not add fake reassurance.
- Do not make Rob sound falsely sentimental.
- Do not imply a work correction is a judgment of Liz personally.
- If an instruction is ambiguous, surface the ambiguity rather than inventing an answer.

**Example**

Original:
> "This isn't what I asked for. We can't keep doing this. Fix it today."

Intent:
> The current version does not match the requirement and must be corrected today.

Suggested:
> "This isn't what I asked for. Fix this version today before moving on, and make sure it matches the requirements we agreed on."

## 27. Learning Mode

The translator should help both users become better communicators over time rather than making them permanently dependent on translation.

Optional features:
- Intent extracted
- Communication standard applied
- What was removed
- What was preserved
- Shorter version
- More direct version
- Warmer version

## 28. Product Philosophy

The translator is not there to protect people from truth.

It is there to make truth easier to communicate accurately and act on.

It should reduce fear, ambiguity, unnecessary sharpness, over-explaining, defensive interpretation, politeness rituals, and hidden disagreement.

It should increase clarity, ownership, confidence, useful feedback, thoughtful disagreement, faster execution, better decisions, and trust.

The ideal output should feel like:

> **"That is exactly what I meant. I just wish I had said it that clearly."**

# Implementation Note for Codex

When building the communication app:

1. Treat this file as the source of truth for transformation behavior.
2. Keep standards and prompt logic separate from UI components.
3. Preserve original messages.
4. Require human review before sending transformed messages.
5. Never expose API keys in client-side code.
6. Make it easy to update this standards file later.
7. If application behavior conflicts with this document, this document wins unless explicitly overridden.
