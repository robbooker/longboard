An essay, mostly about feelings

The basic trade is against yourself.

On greed, process, the disposition effect, and the quietly ridiculous habit of demanding that a perfectly good trade go do something even better.

By Rob Booker Issue 001 · Trading Psychology Filed under Process
Premise The market is the obvious adversary. The other one — the one that does most of the actual damage — is the trader, renegotiating with the trader's own plan, in real time, under the influence of feelings.
On size Emotional neutrality is great, and apparently you have to buy it. The currency is smaller position sizes.

Look, there are basically two ways to lose money trading, and only one of them is interesting.

The interesting way is the cinematic one. You have a thesis about a company, or a currency, or a macro setup, and you put on a position, and the market disagrees with you in a loud, expensive, and ideally well-timed-to-someone-else's book fashion. This is the version that gets written about. This is the version where, afterward, you give a podcast interview about the importance of conviction. This version is fine. This version is, for our purposes, not the problem.

The other way to lose money is much more common, and it goes like this: you are basically right, the trade basically works, and then you, personally, ruin it.

This second category gets less respect than it deserves, mostly because there is no one else to blame. There is no surprise central bank announcement. There is no activist short report. There is no scandal, no flash crash, no unforeseeable third thing. It is just you, alone, at roughly 2:14 in the afternoon, making a small, weird decision that turns a decent day into an unnecessary lesson. (Most days are like this. This is fine. We will get to it.)

If a trade is large enough to make you euphoric on the way up or panicked on the way down, it is too large.

Here is how it usually starts. You buy something. It goes up. You are up $100. Now, $100 is not a life-changing amount of money. But it is also not nothing. If your plan is to make a series of sensible trades and stack acceptable gains over time — which it is, I assume, because otherwise why are we doing any of this — then $100 is actually pretty good. It is, in fact, very hard to go broke taking the profit you intended to take. This is one of the simplest and most insulting facts in trading.

And yet.

And yet, what happens next happens to traders all the time: you become offended by adequacy.

You think, well, sure, $100, that's a number, but maybe it should be $140. Or $180. Or maybe the market, having already done what I asked it to do, owes me a little more of doing what I asked it to do, as a kind of professional courtesy. So you adjust the order. You widen the target. You squeeze. And the market, which I want to stress does not actually care about your feelings, your plans, or your revised ambitions, goes back down.

Now you make nothing. Now $100 is a story you tell about something you almost had. Anyway.

This is, by the way, a totally normal retail instinct. Professionals have it too. The good ones just build scaffolding to keep themselves from indulging it more than once a week. The issue isn't really greed, exactly. Greed is dramatic. The actual issue is more boring than that: it's a failure to define enough in advance. And once you fail to define enough in advance, you end up renegotiating in real time, which is to say, in front of a chart, while feeling things. Real-time negotiations between you and your emotions tend to have a very clear winner.

What the research says, briefly, with apologies

I want to be upfront: the next part is going to involve citing some Nobel laureates, which is something nobody actually wants in a trading essay, but which I am contractually obligated to do because the alternative is to sound like I just made all of this up.

The broad finding from a few decades of behavioral economics is roughly this: people do not evaluate gains and losses in the cool, rational, calculator-shaped way that older textbook finance liked to imagine. When the Nobel committee handed Daniel Kahneman the 2002 economics prize, they noted that his work integrated psychological insights into economics, especially around how humans actually make decisions under uncertainty. This is a polite way of saying that human beings, even very intelligent ones, do not approach risk like tiny rational accountants. They approach risk while bringing memory, ego, fear, relief, pride, regret, and whatever they happened to eat for breakfast.

Useful, slightly humiliating consequence: losses do not feel like the mathematical opposite of gains. A small loss isn't just a minus sign. It can feel like a verdict. It can feel like proof that the idea, the process, or the entire person behind the process is, frankly, suspect. A gain, by contrast, just feels real and locked in the moment you can book it.

So people grab winners fast and postpone losers indefinitely. There is even a name for this, and it is — unfortunately — a great name: the disposition effect. Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman described it in the 1980s. It is the tendency to sell winners too early and ride losers too long. Terrance Odean later went and looked at actual brokerage accounts, and found that real investors really did this, and that the behavior did not produce better future returns. It just produced better feelings in the present.

That is the slightly rude part of the literature. It is not merely saying that investors trade emotionally. It is saying that the emotional trading does not even help. It does not produce better timing. It does not produce better after-tax results. It just feels nicer on a Tuesday.

The trade inside the trade

A lot of trading advice is much more complicated than it needs to be. People want hidden indicators. They want complex frameworks. They want the one weird setup that institutions don't want them to know about. (The institutions are mostly, I'm told, busy worrying about their own internal compliance trainings.) The actual edge, for most people, is more boring than that.

Trade smaller than you want to.

Take the setup you can actually repeat.

Test it going forward, not just backward.

Keep losses small enough that they're annoying, not identity-destroying.

And if a trade gets where you wanted it to go, maybe just let that count.

This is, I admit, less exciting than the mythology of trading. There is no heroism in following the plan and closing the position. Nobody is going to make a movie about a person who compounds modestly and behaves like an adult. But this is, unfortunately, how a lot of money actually gets made. Which is to say: a lot of money gets made by people you would find a little boring at parties.

One useful way to think about it is that every trade is actually two trades stacked on top of each other. There is the market trade, which is the thing you are buying or selling. And there is the psychological trade, which is the constant, exhausting negotiation between your plan and your impulses about your plan.

The market trade might be totally fine. Your entry was reasonable. Your stop was in a sensible place. The setup was something you'd seen work many times before. And then the psychological trade begins, and it goes roughly: you're up a little, should you take it? You're down a little, should you give it room? It moved faster than you expected — does that mean you're brilliant, or just early? It moved slower than you expected — does that mean you're wrong, or just impatient? (You are, on average, just impatient. But it's a fair question. Right?)

Most of the damage retail traders do to themselves is in that second trade. It does not come from lacking some exotic institutional signal. It comes from continuously interfering with a perfectly fine plan because the feelings generated by the plan have become more interesting than the plan itself.

Why small size, basically, fixes this

One way to put it is that every trader has two jobs. The first is to find trades. The second is to manage the much more volatile asset known as their own personality. The second job is much harder. Markets are at least external. You are inside your head all day, with no exit.

And so we arrive, slowly, at the oldest piece of trading advice in the world, which is: trade smaller. Reduce your size until you can think clearly. If a position is large enough to make you euphoric on the way up or panicked on the way down, it is too large. You are no longer running a strategy. The strategy is now running you, and it is going to do an enthusiastic and creative job of it.

Small size buys you emotional neutrality. Emotional neutrality is, I think, the single most underrated thing in this whole field, partly because nothing about it makes you feel cool.

This advice is hard to internalize because many traders secretly believe that effort should look busy. More charts. More entries. More opinions. More urgency. More cleverness. More buttons. The problem is that investing and trading are among the very few human activities in which visible effort can actually be a tax on results. Most of the time, the people doing the most obviously hard work are doing the most subtly destructive work.

There is a related, very human temptation here: to confuse action with control. If I am doing something, then I must be managing the situation. If I am not doing something, then I must be passively hoping. In markets, this is almost always backwards. The person mashing buttons all day may be the least in-control person in the building.

Anyway, this is one reason traders damage their own good ideas: they keep trying to help them. They trim the winner too early because a gain feels fragile. They nurse the loser too long because a loss feels reversible. They add complexity because complexity feels sophisticated. They increase volume because volume feels professional. All of this looks energetic. Most of it is just expensive fidgeting.

The problem with wanting to be right

There's also a status problem hiding inside bad trade management, which I think we don't talk about enough. Realizing a gain feels like closing a case in your favor. Realizing a loss feels like signing a confession. It really does. That's a big part of why losers are so easy to keep around: an unrealized loss is still a story. A realized loss is a fact. Stories are much nicer.

This is also why a lot of otherwise intelligent people quietly drift into the bizarre, semi-mystical idea that the market owes them time. If they wait long enough, maybe the trade comes back. If they add more, maybe their average price improves. If they reinterpret the original setup with a slightly different headline, maybe the original mistake retroactively becomes a high-conviction thesis. Sometimes, inconveniently, this works. Which only makes the habit worse. The market occasionally rewards bad process. That's one of its meaner features. It trains people to confuse survival with skill.

A loss cut quickly can feel stupid right before the market turns around. A loser held too long can feel brilliant right before it becomes a catastrophe. If you judge yourself trade by trade, you can — I promise — learn almost any wrong lesson you want.

What the boring, mature version looks like

People also like backtests too much. Backtests are nice because they take place in the past, where everything has already happened. The past is very accommodating that way. But you cannot trade in the past. The market has a strong, unsentimental preference for making you perform in the present. Forward testing with small size is far less seductive — it is slow, ambiguous, and uncomfortably subject to your own behavior — but it has the unfortunate property of being where the money actually is.

The durable version of trading is not, "discover the perfect system, become rich immediately." Sorry. It is something like: "find one reasonably good thing, do it small, do it consistently, avoid turning small losses into big ones, and try not to sabotage your winners out of boredom or ambition." That is the whole thing. That is a career.

A mature trading process is therefore not especially dramatic. It is repetitive, mildly boring, and intentionally constrained. It defines what counts as an entry, what counts as an exit, what counts as a stop, and what counts as a size worth taking. It also accepts a slightly humiliating truth: the system is partly designed to protect the trader from the trader.

That doesn't mean becoming robotic in some grandiose way. It just means acknowledging that discretion is expensive when exercised under stress. The best traders are usually not the ones with the most brilliant intuitions in real time. They're the ones who already know which decisions should have been made before real time arrived.

Which brings us back to where we started. A good trade that's up a little does not need your ambition. A bad trade that's down a little does not need your wounded pride. Both trades, frankly, need approximately the same thing from you, which is for you to stop improvising.

The market does not require brilliance from most people.

It mostly requires that you stop doing extra stuff.
