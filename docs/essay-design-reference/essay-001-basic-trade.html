An essay on process & restraint

The basic trade is against yourself.

A long-form post about greed, process, and the quietly destructive habit of asking a good trade to become a perfect one.

By Rob Booker Issue 001 · Trading Psychology Filed under Process
Premise The market is the obvious adversary. The less obvious one is the trader's own renegotiation, in real time, with their own plan.
On size Emotional neutrality, the essay argues, is bought — and the currency is smaller position size.

There are, broadly speaking, two ways to lose money trading.

The first is the glamorous way, where you have a thesis about a company, or a currency, or a macro setup, and the market disagrees with you in a loud and expensive fashion.

The second is the more common way, which is that you are basically right, the trade basically works, and then you personally ruin it.

If a trade is large enough to make you euphoric on the way up or panicked on the way down, it is too large.

This second category gets less respect than it deserves. It is not cinematic. There is no surprise central bank announcement, no activist short report, no scandal, no flash crash. It is just you, alone, making a small weird decision that turns a decent day into an unnecessary lesson.

Suppose you buy something. It goes up. You are up $100. This is not a life-changing amount of money, but it is also not nothing. In fact, if your plan is to make a series of sensible trades and stack acceptable gains over time, then $100 is pretty good. It is very hard to go broke taking the profit you intended to take.

But then something happens that happens to traders all the time: you become offended by adequacy.

You think, well, if I am up $100, perhaps I should be up $140. Or $180. Or perhaps the market, having already done what I asked, should continue doing more of it as a gesture of respect. So you adjust the order, widen the target, squeeze a little more, and the market, which does not care about your feelings, your plans, or your revised ambitions, goes back down. Now you make nothing.

This is a common retail instinct. Professionals have it too, but the good ones build systems to keep themselves from indulging it too often. The issue is not really greed, exactly. It is a failure to define enough in advance. If you do not decide ahead of time what outcome counts as success, you will renegotiate in real time, and real-time negotiations between you and your emotions tend to have a clear winner.

What the research says

Economists and psychologists have spent decades trying to explain why people behave this way around gains and losses. The modern answer starts with a broad idea and then gets progressively more annoying, because the broad idea is intuitive and the details are uncomfortably familiar.

The broad idea is that people do not evaluate outcomes in the cool detached way textbook finance once liked to imagine. In awarding Daniel Kahneman the 2002 economics prize, the Nobel committee said his work integrated insights from psychology into economics, especially around judgment and decision-making under uncertainty. That is a polite way of saying that human beings do not approach risk as tiny rational calculators. They bring memory, ego, fear, relief, pride, regret, and whatever else they happened to eat for breakfast.

One of the most useful consequences of that research is the recognition that losses often feel different from gains in ways that distort behavior. A small loss is not just a negative number. It can feel like a verdict. It can feel like proof that the idea, the process, or the person behind the process is flawed. A gain, meanwhile, feels satisfyingly real the moment it can be locked in. So people are tempted to grab winners quickly and postpone the emotional pain of realizing losers.

This tendency has a name in finance: the disposition effect. Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman described it in the 1980s as the tendency to sell winners too early and ride losers too long. Terrance Odean later tested it in brokerage data and found that investors showed a strong preference for realizing winners rather than losers, and that the behavior was not justified by subsequent performance.

That is the slightly rude part of the research. It is not merely saying that investors behave emotionally. It is saying that the emotional behavior often does not even help on its own terms. It does not reliably produce better after-tax results, better timing, or better future returns. It simply feels better in the moment.

The trade inside the trade

A lot of trading advice is more complicated than it needs to be. People want hidden indicators, complex frameworks, the one weird setup that institutions do not want you to know about. But a lot of the actual edge is more boring.

Trade smaller than you want to.

Take the setup you can actually repeat.

Test it going forward, not just backward.

Keep losses small enough that they are annoying rather than identity-destroying.

And if the trade gets where you wanted it to go, maybe just let that count.

This is less exciting than the mythology of trading. There is no heroism in following the plan and closing the position. Nobody makes a movie about a person who compounds modestly and behaves like an adult. But this is, unfortunately, how a lot of money gets made.

One useful way to think about this is that every trade actually contains two trades. There is the market trade, which is the thing you are buying or selling. Then there is the psychological trade, which is the constant negotiation between your plan and your impulses.

The market trade might be fine. Your entry might be reasonable. Your stop might be in the right place. The setup might be one you have seen work many times before. And then the psychological trade begins. You are up a little. Should you take it? You are down a little. Should you give it room? It moved faster than you expected. Does that mean you are brilliant, or just early? It moved slower than you expected. Does that mean you are wrong, or just impatient?

Most of the damage retail traders do to themselves happens in that second trade. It does not come from lacking access to some exotic institution-only signal. It comes from repeatedly interfering with the plan because the feelings generated by the plan become more important than the plan itself.

Why small size changes everything

One way to think about it is that every trader has two jobs. One is to find trades. The other is to manage the much more volatile asset known as their own personality. The second job is often harder. Markets are chaotic, but at least they are external. You have to live inside your own head all day.

And this is why the oldest advice is often still the best. Reduce your size until you can think clearly. If a position is large enough to make you euphoric on the way up or panicked on the way down, it is too large. You are no longer running a strategy; the strategy is running you. Small size buys you emotional neutrality, and emotional neutrality is underrated.

This is useful because many traders secretly believe that effort should look busy. More charts. More entries. More opinions. More urgency. More interventions. More cleverness. But investing and trading are among the few domains where visible effort can be a tax on results.

There is a standard human temptation to equate action with control. If I am doing something, then I am managing the situation. If I am not doing something, then I am passively hoping for the best. In markets this can invert. The person mashing buttons all day may be the least in control person in the room.

In that sense, one reason traders damage good ideas is that they keep trying to help them. They trim the winner too early because a gain feels fragile. They nurse the loser too long because a loss feels reversible. They add complexity because complexity feels sophisticated. They increase volume because volume feels professional. All of this can look energetic and committed. Much of it is just expensive fidgeting.

The problem with wanting to be right

There is also a status problem hidden inside bad trade management. Realizing a gain feels like closing a case in your own favor. Realizing a loss feels like signing a confession. That is one reason losers are so easy to keep around. An unrealized loss is still a story. A realized loss is a fact.

This is why a lot of otherwise intelligent people drift into the bizarre idea that the market owes them time. If they wait long enough, maybe the trade comes back. If they add more, maybe their average price improves. If they reinterpret the setup, maybe the original mistake becomes a higher-conviction thesis. Sometimes, inconveniently, this works. Which only makes the habit more dangerous.

The market occasionally rewards bad process. That is one of its meaner features. It trains people to confuse survival with skill. A loss cut quickly can feel stupid right before the market turns around. A loser held too long can feel brilliant right before it becomes a catastrophe. If you judge yourself trade by trade, you can learn almost any wrong lesson you want.

What a mature process looks like

Also, people like backtests too much. Backtests are nice because they occur in the past, where everything is knowable. But you cannot trade in the past. The market has a strong preference for making you perform in the present. Forward testing with small size is less seductive, because it is slow and uncertain and happens in reality. But reality is, inconveniently, where the money is.

Anyway the durable version of trading is not: discover the perfect system and become rich immediately.

It is more like: find one reasonably good thing, do it small, do it consistently, avoid turning small losses into huge ones, and try not to sabotage your winners out of boredom or ambition.

A mature trading process is therefore not especially dramatic. It is repetitive, mildly boring, and intentionally constrained. It defines what constitutes an entry, what constitutes an exit, what constitutes a stop, and what constitutes a size worth taking. It also accepts a humiliating truth: the system is partly designed to protect the trader from the trader.

That does not mean becoming robotic in a grandiose way. It means acknowledging that discretion is expensive when exercised under stress. The best traders are often not those with the most brilliant intuitions in real time. They are the ones who know which decisions should have been made before real time ever arrived.

And that returns us to the original problem. A good trade that is up a little does not need your ambition. A bad trade that is down a little does not need your wounded pride. Both trades mostly need the same thing: for you to stop improvising.

The market does not actually require brilliance from most people.

It mostly requires that you stop doing extra stuff.
