An essay on size & overconfidence

Trade smaller than your ego likes.

On position sizing, overconfidence, and why being too big too early ruins more traders than bad ideas do.

By Rob Booker Issue 003 · Position Sizing Filed under Process
Premise Most traders don't begin with a good strategy and scale into it carefully. They begin with enthusiasm, add too much size, and discover that emotion has become their largest position.
The right order Reverse it. Trade small enough to think clearly. Repeat the setup until you actually know it. Then increase size gradually.

Most traders do not begin with a good strategy and then scale into it carefully. They begin with enthusiasm, add too much size, and then discover that emotion has become their largest position.

The order is usually backwards

There is a very common sequence in trading, and it goes like this: someone becomes excited about a setup, trades it too large, feels too much, behaves badly, takes damage, and only afterward becomes interested in discipline.

This is a terrible way to learn because the lesson arrives after the capital has already paid tuition. The right order is almost exactly the reverse. First trade small enough to think clearly. Then repeat the setup enough times to learn what it actually does in live conditions. Then, only then, increase size gradually.

Traders hate this advice because it sounds insulting. The ego wants to believe that conviction is evidence. If I feel strongly enough, perhaps that means I see something meaningful. If I am excited enough, perhaps that means the opportunity is unusually good. Usually it means only that the story is appealing.

Why size is a behavioral problem

Position size is often discussed as a mechanical question, almost a matter of spreadsheet hygiene. But a large part of sizing is not mathematical. It is psychological. The size of a position determines the emotional intensity of the trade, and emotional intensity changes decision quality.

When a trade is small enough, you can observe it. You can notice slippage, timing, how often the setup appears, whether your exits are too optimistic, whether your stop is in the wrong place, whether the whole idea only looked good in theory. In other words, you can learn.

When a trade is too large, learning ends. The mind stops gathering information and starts defending the self.

You are no longer asking, "What is this trade teaching me?" You are asking, "How fast can I stop feeling this?"

Small size preserves curiosity.

Oversized trades produce autobiography.

Overconfidence tends to show up in size

The research on investor overconfidence usually talks about high turnover and excessive trading, but there is a quieter and arguably more revealing clue: position size. John Forman and Joanne Horton studied retail trading and argued that relative position size may be a better indicator of overconfidence than sheer trading frequency. Their finding, in plain English, is not flattering. Traders taking relatively larger positions tended to make more impaired timing decisions.

This is intuitively sensible. A trader can be busy without being especially confident. But taking unusually large size is a direct statement about belief. It says, not only do I think this idea is good, I think it is good enough to deserve disproportionate capital.

That is why size often functions as the purest expression of ego in markets. It reveals what you think you know before the market has had a chance to correct you.

The fantasy of the big swing

Traders also oversize because they are impatient. The small-trade path is deeply uncinematic. Nobody wants the montage where the hero takes careful notes, compounds modestly, and slowly builds trust in a repeatable process. People want the moment where they "step on the gas."

Sometimes stepping on the gas works. This is one of the reasons bad habits survive. The occasional huge win gives moral cover to a fragile process. But a process should not be judged by how dramatic its best day was. It should be judged by whether it remains functional under ordinary conditions.

Size that is justified only by urgency is usually a confession. It means the trader does not trust time to do the work.

The boring argument for staying small

There is an old and slightly humbling idea embedded in risk management literature: survival matters more than theatrics. Edward Thorp's work on growth and bankroll management made famous the idea that wager size is inseparable from long-term survival. The exact formulas are less important here than the principle. If your sizing policy is careless, even an edge can be destroyed.

That principle is often diluted in retail trading culture because formulas are less seductive than certainty. But the core point remains. Capital allocation is not a decoration on top of a strategy. It is one of the strategy's main gears.

This is also why the traders who last often look unimpressive from the outside. They are not necessarily the most aggressive or the most active. They are often the ones who find a setup they can actually execute, size it in a way that lets them stay rational, and then let time do the compounding.

How to know if you are too big

You are too big if every tick feels personal.

You are too big if your stop feels humiliating instead of normal.

You are too big if one losing trade changes your mood for the rest of the day.

You are too big if your planning becomes more theatrical as the trade moves against you.

You are too big if, instead of observing the setup, you begin bargaining with it.

This is not a moral failure. It is useful information. It means your current size is generating more emotion than your process can absorb.

The professional version

Professionalism in trading is sometimes misunderstood as bravery. It is usually closer to calibration. The mature trader is not the one who can tolerate the most pain. It is the one who understands how little pain is required to learn something useful.

That is why "trade smaller than your ego likes" is not timid advice. It is infrastructure advice. It is how you convert a hunch into a process, a process into evidence, and evidence into something worth scaling.

Trade small enough to stay intelligent.

Then earn the right to become larger.
